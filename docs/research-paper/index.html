Detecting and Correcting Synthetic-Data Overfitting in a Multi-Model Customer Feedback Triage Pipeline

Pranav Chaturvedi
OmniFeedback AI Project  ·  github.com/achellesheel/omnifeedback-ai
Abstract

We present OmniFeedback AI, a feedback-triage system combining a transactional warehouse, K-Means clustering, an urgency regressor, transformer NER/summarization, and a GenAI copilot. We document two sequential post-deployment corrections. V1→V2: an initial corpus yielded suspiciously perfect metrics (F1=1.000, silhouette=1.000) — an artifact of 9 unique training sentences, not competence — fixed with a hardened generator and a Kneedle elbow method replacing naive silhouette-maximizing K-selection. V2→V3: a user of the deployed app found the resulting BiLSTM scoring natural critical and positive feedback almost identically; we trace this to a 196-word from-scratch vocabulary (42–55% OOV on natural text) and correct it via partial transfer learning — a fine-tuned DistilBERT, 21.4% of parameters trainable — yielding a ~13× larger critical-vs-low score separation on hand-written text with no additional data. We further validate this correction on 150 examples/class of real, independently-authored Yelp and Sentiment140 text: V3's separation remains statistically significant on both (p<0.0001) at roughly half the hand-written magnitude, and its correlation with Yelp's full 5-class rating (Spearman ρ=0.682) is markedly stronger than V2's (ρ=0.073, not significant). We report full before/after metrics for both fixes, an error analysis, a null channel–urgency result (p=0.84) traced to generator design, a checkpoint-size engineering note, and an explicit characterization of what the corrected model's CRITICAL threshold actually detects. All three configurations remain live and independently reproducible.

1Introduction

Enterprises triage feedback across uncoordinated channels — reviews, tickets, social posts — too slowly to catch emerging incidents. Automated triage combines clustering, regression-based urgency scoring, and LLM-based drafting, each typically validated in isolation on whatever data is on hand. When that data is synthetic, near-perfect offline metrics can reflect trivial separability rather than generalizable competence — a failure mode this paper documents concretely.

We trace that failure as it occurred in OmniFeedback AI: the diagnosis, two applied fixes, and the resulting metrics. Both the naive (V1) and hardened (V2) configurations remain live and re-runnable, so Table 1 is reproducible rather than merely asserted.

2Related Work

Bidirectional LSTMs [1,2] remain a strong, low-resource baseline for sequence regression relative to transformer fine-tuning. K-Means selection commonly relies on the silhouette coefficient [3]; the Kneedle algorithm [4] formalizes elbow detection as maximum distance from the chord joining a curve's endpoints — applied here to a "higher is better" metric rather than its original inertia framing. NER and summarization use BERT [5] and BART [6]; Section 6's transfer-learning correction fine-tunes DistilBERT [7], a distilled encoder retaining most of BERT's language understanding at a fraction of its parameter count. The copilot draws on chain-of-thought [8] and few-shot prompting [9], optimized with Adam [10].

4Methodology

4.1 Two generator variants

V1 (naive): 9 unique phrases (5 negative, 4 positive) sampled at a flat 40% negative rate across 4,000 records. V2 (hardened): templated, randomized phrasing (212 unique surface forms), an ambiguous class straddling the 0.5 threshold, and ~3% label noise. Both share identical downstream code, isolating any metric difference to the data alone.

4.2 Cluster-count selection

Figure 2. Silhouette vs. k. V1 saturates at 1.0 once k reaches the corpus's unique-string count; V2 shows a genuine, gradually diminishing curve. Naive argmax silently selects the search boundary on V1.

Naive argmax(silhouette) over k∈[2,12] scores 1.000 for every k≥9 on V1 (Fig. 2) — clusters of exact duplicates, not signal. We replace it with Kneedle: normalize both axes, select k at maximum perpendicular distance from the endpoint chord.

4.3 Training protocol

A single fixed-seed 70/15/15 split precedes training. The BiLSTM (embed 128 → BiLSTM 64/dir → dropout 0.3 → sigmoid) trains under Adam (decay 1e-5) with MSE loss and early stopping on validation loss (patience=4); all metrics are test-split only.

6V3: A Transfer-Learning Correction

Post-deployment, a person testing the live application (not any automated test) found that V2 scored natural-language critical and positive feedback almost identically whenever phrasing diverged from the training templates. This section documents the diagnosis and fix as a second, independent case study in the same spirit as Section 4.2's elbow-selection correction: a suspicious result, investigated to a measurable root cause, fixed, and verified — this time a generalization failure rather than a data-construction artifact.

6.1 Diagnosis: a 196-word closed vocabulary

Figure 6. Out-of-vocabulary rate: text matching V2's training templates vs. natural-language critical/positive sentences. At 42–55% OOV, roughly half of each sentence is invisible to a model whose vocabulary was learned entirely from ~2,800 training rows.

V2's BiLSTM (§4.3) learns its embedding table from scratch; its vocabulary — words occurring ≥2 times in the training split — is 196 tokens. Tokenizing natural stress-test sentences against it showed 42–55% out-of-vocabulary. With roughly half the input mapped to a single <UNK> token, the model retains little signal and its output regresses toward the training mean irrespective of true severity.

6.2 Correction: partial transfer learning

We replace the from-scratch embedding with a fine-tuned distilbert-base-uncased encoder, freezing the embeddings and first 4 of 6 transformer layers and training only the last 2 layers plus a linear regression head — 21.4% of parameters trainable (14.2M / 66.4M), deliberately mirroring a partial-fine-tuning ratio observed to outperform from-scratch training in an unrelated image-classification task. Subword tokenization eliminates the closed-vocabulary failure mode entirely: any English string decomposes into known subword units regardless of training-corpus coverage.

6.3 Results: unchanged in-distribution, corrected out-of-distribution

Figure 7. Mean urgency score by expected severity, V2 vs. V3, on the same 8 out-of-distribution sentences. V2's critical/low separation (0.022) is statistically indistinguishable from noise; V3's (0.298) is not — a ~13× increase with no additional training data.

On the held-out synthetic test split, V2 and V3 are comparable (MSE 0.0162 vs. 0.0160; macro-F1 0.821 vs. 0.825) — expected, since that split is drawn from the same template family both models trained on and cannot itself reveal an out-of-distribution generalization gap. On 8 natural-language sentences constructed to fall outside those templates, V2's critical-minus-low mean-score separation is 0.022; V3's is 0.298 — a difference this large is the finding, not the in-distribution parity above.

6.4 An engineering note: checkpoint size

The naively-saved full model checkpoint is 265MB, exceeding GitHub's 100MB per-file limit. Since ~79% of that size is the frozen, unmodified pretrained backbone — recoverable at load time via the same from_pretrained() call already used for this project's NER/summarization components — we persist only the fine-tuned parameter subset (56.7MB) and reconstruct the frozen weights on load, verified to reproduce bit-identical predictions to the full checkpoint before adopting the change.

6.5 Honest boundary: what the CRITICAL threshold actually detects

Testing ~20 additional natural-language sentences against V3 found its ≥0.8 CRITICAL threshold is not a general severity scale: sentences describing severe operational impact alone (e.g., extended production downtime with revenue loss) reliably score 0.69–0.79 (HIGH) without crossing 0.8. Two patterns reliably do: data-deletion-plus-immediate-fix-demand phrasing (0.86) and unauthorized-account-access-plus-financial-risk phrasing (0.81) — both closely matching specific template families in V2's training data that V3 inherited via its training labels. This is expected behavior for a threshold calibrated to a training distribution, not a defect, but it is a genuine limit on what "CRITICAL" means in this system that we state explicitly rather than leave for a user to discover unassisted.

6.6 External validation: does the fix replicate on independent real text?

Figure 8. Critical-minus-low separation, V2 vs. V3, on 150 examples/class of real Yelp reviews and Sentiment140 tweets (proxy labels: 1-star/negative = critical, 5-star/positive = low). Both V3 bars are Mann-Whitney significant at p<0.0001; V2's Yelp bar is significant but small (p=0.0048); V2's Sentiment140 bar is not significant (p=0.39).

§6.1–6.5 used 8 hand-written sentences — sufficient to demonstrate the failure mode but vulnerable to the objection that curated examples inflate the apparent fix. We re-ran the identical separation metric on 150 real examples per class from Yelp Review Full (proxy label: star rating) and Sentiment140 (proxy label: binary sentiment), neither authored by us. V3's separation remains significant on both (Yelp 0.127, Sentiment140 0.084; both p<0.0001 by Mann-Whitney U), confirming the fix generalizes beyond curated text — but at roughly half the magnitude of the hand-written result (0.30), indicating real text is measurably harder to separate than examples written to be unambiguous. A secondary result: V2's separation is not uniformly null — it is small but statistically significant on Yelp's longer reviews (p=0.0048) yet indistinguishable from noise on Sentiment140's short tweets (p=0.39), consistent with §6.1's mechanism (shorter text leaves proportionally less in-vocabulary signal). A Spearman correlation against Yelp's full 5-class rating gives the most granular comparison available: V3 ρ=0.682 (p<0.0001) versus V2 ρ=0.073 (p=0.14, not significant) — V2 shows no reliable monotonic relationship with real sentiment across the full ordinal scale, while V3 does.

7Discussion

7.1 Error composition

V2 false negatives cluster almost entirely in the deliberately-ambiguous class (target 0.35–0.6) — a boundary miss, not a comprehension failure. One false positive traces directly to the label-noise injection on a canonically critical sentence, meaning the model was semantically correct against a deliberately corrupted target.

7.2 A metric-mismatch pitfall

An initial threshold sweep optimized precision_recall_curve's positive-class F1, not the macro-F1 used elsewhere — yielding a "best" threshold that scored worse than the shipped default, a logical contradiction that only became visible because the numbers didn't add up.

7.3 Independence as a data limitation

The null channel result reflects the generator's independent metadata assignment, not evidence that channel is generally uninformative — a distinction worth stating plainly rather than treating a synthetic design choice as a finding about the world.

7.4 Deployment under CPU-only, memory-constrained hosting

Two incidents surfaced only under real hosted deployment, not local testing. First, a memory-ceiling crash ("gone over its resource limits") traced — via HuggingFace's file-metadata API rather than inference — to three transformer models resident simultaneously (bert-large-cased-conll03, 1.33GB; bart-large-cnn, 1.63GB; the V3 DistilBERT backbone), ≈2.96GB combined with no GPU. Resolved by substituting smaller same-task models (bert-base-NER, 433MB; distilbart-cnn-12-6, 1.22GB), reducing the combined NER+summarization footprint to ≈1.66GB, with output quality re-verified at both single-record and full production-batch scale before the smaller models were accepted as a replacement, not merely assumed equivalent from parameter count alone. Second, a CPU-throttling event followed under sustained inference load — an expected consequence of running four transformer-scale models on a free CPU-only tier rather than a defect in the code; documented as a hosting-tier constraint rather than papered over with a workaround that does not exist at this service tier.

7.5 A summarization architecture mismatch

Batch executive summaries produced by the BART/DistilBART pipeline (§3, §6.6's summarizer) were reported as reading close to extractive on this corpus — reusing ticket phrasing nearly verbatim rather than synthesizing cross-ticket themes. This is consistent with BART's pretraining regime (span corruption and reconstruction over long-form CNN/DailyMail news prose): on short, already-terse, keyword-dense complaint sentences, there is comparatively little redundant prose to compress, so the model's salient-span selection surfaces directly as near-verbatim reuse. No parameter-count variant within the BART family addresses this, since the behavior follows from the pretraining objective and input register, not model capacity. We extended the existing pluggable-backend pattern used for the resolution copilot (§3): an instruction-following LLM (Claude), given an explicit synthesis-oriented system prompt directing it to name recurring themes rather than reuse ticket sentences, drafts the brief when an API key is configured; the BART pipeline remains the zero-configuration fallback, labeled in the interface as more extractive on this register rather than presented as equivalent.

8Limitations

§6.6 validates V3's separation on real Yelp/Sentiment140 text, but only for the binary critical-vs-low comparison; the two narrow CRITICAL-triggering patterns identified in §6.5 were not re-tested against real data, and real data would plausibly broaden them. The copilot's local fallback is rule-based, not learned, and untested here against its optional Claude-backed path. V3 corrects the urgency regressor only; the TF-IDF-based clustering and baseline classifiers retain the same closed-vocabulary property that motivated Section 6 and have not yet received an equivalent correction or external validation. The NER component (§3, §7.4) is trained on the CoNLL-2003 tag scheme, which defines exactly four entity types (PER/ORG/LOC/MISC) with no dedicated product or software-version category; product mentions ("iPhone 15", "iOS 17.2") are consequently tagged MISC by construction rather than by model error, a limitation shared by essentially all CoNLL-trained NER models regardless of parameter count. An OntoNotes-trained alternative would add a PRODUCT tag, but the small candidates checked were not reliably available and the one confirmed-available option is a ~1.7GB Flair model — larger than this system's entire current NER+summarization footprint (§7.4) and not adopted for that reason; this is reported as a considered trade-off rather than an oversight. The Claude-backed summarization path in §7.5 was verified only for control flow and output shape (backend selection, schema conformance) via a substituted response, not for the actual synthesis quality of live model output, since no API credential was available in this evaluation environment — a claim of improved semantic quality for that path specifically therefore awaits end-to-end verification with a live key.

9Conclusion

We documented several sequential diagnostic cycles — suspicious metrics traced to a data-construction artifact, a live-deployment failure traced to a closed-vocabulary generalization gap, resource-constrained hosting failures traced to measured model memory footprints rather than assumed ones, and a summarization-quality complaint traced to a mismatch between pretraining objective and input register — each resolved (or, where a measured trade-off argued against a fix, explicitly documented as such) with a verifiable, measured basis inside a system where all three model configurations stay live specifically so every table and figure here is reproducible, not asserted.
